# Are We Becoming the Audience Again?

When you don't exercise, your muscles shrink, and the same happens to your brain. When we have to focus on something and force ourselves to work on something hard, we train the connections between our neurons and make them stronger. Recently I started hearing more and more about the term "brain rot", which means a slowly dying or shrinking brain, and there is a lot of truth to that. Neural networks were sort of inspired by our own brain, and now, ironically, we are making these artificial brains stronger. We keep using them more and more, while delegating all of the hard work we used to do on our own to our artificial twins. And to be fair, I have also felt this way quite a lot lately!

## A Quick Retrospective

I got my first computer at the age of 5, and even though it was already an old piece of tech back then, it opened up a completely new world for me. Before I got my computer, my main interactions with electronics were a Walkman (if anyone here knows what that was) and the TV. Both were quite one-way, or passive, let's call it that. You could only listen and watch, but there was almost no interaction with these devices. Lego, puzzles, and my constant dreams of building a treehouse (and my attempts at it) were my options at that age to put myself in the builder's space. When I first got my hands on a computer, for the first time I felt like I could interact with the device instead of being passive. Of course, it was mainly about playing video games and maybe drawing in Paint, but with time these things led me all the way to basic programming. Nevertheless, the point was creativity. I could finally create something, and do it in a virtual world, without needing physical resources. I could also finally become part of the story, instead of just being a passive watcher. Not to mention that computers also taught me a lot of things, including the English I am using right now to write this blog.

## The Propaganda

With limited sources of information, people back then could mainly get their information from a small number of TV channels, the radio, and some printed media. That's all! The rest could only be learned either by travelling, which was a bit less accessible back in the 90s and before, or through word of mouth. With such limited options, it was much easier to steer and manipulate how people think and what information they get.

Then we got access to computers and the Internet.

Shortly after came forums and social media, which of course brought their good and bad sides. Still, with all of these tools, an improving economic situation around the world, easier travel and so on, people could finally be less prone to manipulation by the mass media. We could share our thoughts openly, share our experiences, and get information about the world from various sources, including independent ones. On the other hand, the Big Brother vision from Orwell's *1984* started to become more and more realistic, with more surveillance tools in place than ever.

So how does artificial slop help propaganda? Even though I feel like I am nearly always up to date with the newest tech, and I work with the newest AI tools on a daily basis, I still sometimes find myself fooled by AI-generated videos, music, and other content. It's still not that difficult to spot, but it's becoming harder and harder. Just look at the famous Will Smith AI evolution video. And even if you don't fall into the trap of AI-generated videos or any other content, because we delegate more and more to AI, some false information can slip into content that is supposed to be genuine.

## The Attention Span Crisis

Various sources claim that the average adult's attention span on a screen fell from about 2.5 minutes in 2004 to around 47 seconds today. That's more than 3 times lower, in just about 20 years! Looking at the world around us, it actually makes sense. Even when I was a kid, only a few people had a mobile phone, and I remember my grandma and my mother having long calls on the landline. There were no messaging apps, no mobile phones, no SMS. To call someone, you would either need to get home or use a phone booth. The same goes for media. There was no social media, and we used to watch TV programmes or films on VHS cassettes. Of course, on TV you would get interrupted by ads, yet that was still probably one of the very few distractions at the time.

Fast forward to 2026: nearly everyone has a mobile phone, we can reach anyone in seconds, calls and messages cost almost nothing, and you can also reach people at scale more easily than ever.

I can only imagine that working back in the early 2000s was somewhat easier on focus. You would probably only get a random distraction, either from a phone call or from a colleague asking you for something. Today, you get to work, and unless your phone is in "Do Not Disturb" mode or completely away, you will most likely get tons of notifications, starting with your private messaging apps, going through emails, and finishing with various social media notifications.

That's one thing. Another is how we spend our time. When we are tired after work and don't want to force our brain to work, we may be tempted to grab the phone and scroll through social media shorts to watch funny cat videos and the like. Or maybe you even go to YouTube and look up history videos, which bring more value. The question is: how does all of this affect our brain? And do we need all of this information? On the one hand, it's nice that knowledge is so broadly available and democratised, but doesn't it create a FOMO effect, where we are constantly tempted to click on another video or learn about something else? I personally struggle with it, and even though I avoid short-form social media apps, I still fall into the trap of "extra" knowledge by watching history or science videos on YouTube. What's worse, we get recommended videos on the side, which creates yet another temptation to click and see what's there, as if we were about to lose a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity.

How does all of this affect our brain, with the constant context switching and the endless flow of information? How long can we focus on one topic to really, fully understand it?

## The Outsourcing Fallacy

That brings us back to the topic of AI. On the one hand, we can finally do things 10x faster, or put any number you want here. People (or machines?) can produce or generate way more output, and way faster, than ever. Nearly all of the world's knowledge is democratised and accessible to anyone who has access to the Internet.

For the last 20 years, our attention span has been attacked by constant notifications from smart devices, by being "connected" to the world, and by the social media effect.

One thing still required some focus and deep work from us, and that was mostly our work: abstract thinking and creative work in the metaphysical space in general. For me, it was programming. Even though the code was always only a tool, it was a sort of unique skill. It not only gave me a way of trading my time for money and the resources to live, but also put me on the market with a unique set of skills that let me talk to computers, and therefore help people build solutions. That also required a lot of time investment on my side, but it was also a sort of game. I remember days when I worked on Codewars challenges, or simply tried to find a way to build some app or fix a bug. Sometimes I felt horrible for days when I couldn't find the solution, and when the moment of "enlightenment" came, it was like a blessing, and it gave me the satisfaction of doing the work. Yet all of these are secondary things. The primary advantage of that work was that I simply had to focus on it and train my brain muscle.

Programming is just one example, though. A huge part of the global population has worked in front of computers for the last few decades, and still does. It's purely intellectual work that is mostly intangible, but still has to be done. It can be proofreading, writing, content creation, or maybe even filling in Excel sheets. Today, most of the work we as humans do in front of computers can be almost fully automated. With tools such as Codex computer use, it reaches Jarvis level, where the computer can act 1:1 just like a human working on it.

It goes far beyond that. I sometimes even find myself too lazy to go through a long article, and I just ask AI to create a summary of it for me instead. This also extends to meeting notes, critical thinking about problems, and so on.

## The Loop?

Taking all of the above into consideration, are we in a loop? And no, I don't mean the self-improving AI loop, but rather a historical cycle: for decades we humans were mostly passengers, passively consuming content, then we got access to tools we had never had before, and now we are moving back to becoming the audience again?

## Are We All Doomed, Then?

Recently I visited Athens, and the place not only inspired me but also made me think a lot. How come so many thinkers, scientists, and artists came from that place at around the same time? Why Athens and not other places, and why that particular time? Well, the era of the great thinkers of that region came around the same time the Greeks were quite powerful and rich. I also learned that only around 10% of the population of Athens were Greek citizens of working age, and there were many more slaves from other countries who did the mundane tasks. As a result, the rich local Greeks had more time than ever to just wander around, explore the world, do research, think, and reflect.

It's just a theory, but maybe it's all thanks to the fact that they simply had the time and capacity to think abstractly, think outside the box, and explore what can be done beyond what we already know? We still use some of their findings to this day. Just think about a time when you had a lot of work, then came back home to look after your family, clean, do the shopping, etc. How much capacity, time, and energy did you have left for pure research and experiments?

On the other hand, it's also quite sad that behind all of these successes there was most likely the hard work and sacrificed lives of so many people. Without them, we couldn't have had these noble figures, as they themselves would likely not have had the time and capacity to discover new things. It's quite a provocative question, but maybe that's also the reason why, to this day, the biggest thinkers and the people who change the world come from rich families?

Why did I even mention this? Maybe it all gives us a little bit of hope, but also a lesson. We are slowly regaining the time that used to be spent on lots of mundane tasks, and now we can reinvest that time to level up as a civilisation. For me, the search goes on. How about you?
